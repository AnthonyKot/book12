Part II · The past · Chapter 3

Empire & slavery

An applicant in Copenhagen preparing for the winter citizenship exam downloads the ministry's textbook: 243 pages, and on page 17, Danish forts on the Gold Coast, over 100,000 people shipped west to islands Denmark would later sell. An applicant in Canberra downloads 50 pages in which nobody is shipped anywhere: the word "colonisation" does not appear, and the country begins as a solution to prison overcrowding. Same exam, different continent, same question on the form: tell me about your past. This chapter reads eight answers.

The question · the load-bearing verb

When a million people are moved against their will, who acts in the sentence a state writes for its newest citizens?

1 What happened

  • Between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries, European states shipped on the order of twelve million enslaved Africans across the Atlantic. Britain and Portugal carried the most; France, Denmark, Spain and the Netherlands all ran the trade under their own flags.
  • Abolition came in stages and in different orders: Denmark banned its trade from 1803 but kept slavery legal in its islands until 1848; Britain banned the trade in 1807 and slavery in the Empire in 1833; France abolished slavery in 1794, saw it re-established in 1802, and abolished it again in 1848; the United States by constitutional amendment in 1865.
  • The documents in this chapter are the current official citizenship-test study materials of eight states (editions in Method and the receipts below). Five are narrative booklets; three are question banks or hybrids — a genre difference the Limits section weighs.
  • Every state in this chapter's ladder either ran the transatlantic trade, was built as a settler colony, or both.

2 The ladder

Eight self-portraits, ordered by how the sentence handles the crime: from a named crime with a number and a law at the top, down through ledger entries, redemption arcs and deleted agents, to silence — and, at the very bottom, silence with a recognized genocide standing outside the frame. 3 The tell

Read the ladder again, verbs only: reconnaît — a state recognizing, in the present tense, with a statute number. Blev sejlet, blev forbudt, blev solgt — sailed, banned, sold, the whole empire in the Danish passive, closing with real estate: the islands "were sold to the USA in 1917." Became the first province in the Empire to move toward abolition — Canada entering its own slavery chapter already on the winning side. Freed the slaves — Britain too, with the Royal Navy as the story's policeman. Was taken — nobody takes. Then the verbs stop.

"After 1833, 2 million Indian and Chinese workers were employed to replace the freed slaves."

UK · Life in the United Kingdom, 3rd ed. 2013 · one verb carrying indentured labour

The British sentence above is the chapter's cleanest specimen, because every word in it is doing assigned work. "Employed" converts indenture into a job offer. "Replace" concedes, in passing, that the plantation economy did not stop — only the word for its workforce changed. And the paragraph's opening move — slavery "was illegal within Britain itself" — draws the border around the crime scene so that the nation stands outside it. Canada opens with the same compass: "Slavery has existed all over the world" — everyone did it — before narrowing, within two sentences, to the one jurisdiction that moved first to stop it. Neither book invents a fact. Both arrange the facts into an alibi.

The two zeros are not the same zero. Spain's manual celebrates the empire ("the first empire with great expanses of territory on every continent") and omits what it ran on; but on page 71, in the residence table, the empire quietly reappears as law — two years' wait for Bolivians and Sephardic Jews, ten for everyone else. The history it won't narrate, it still administers: the fast track is a remedy for an expulsion the manual never mentions. Australia's zero is more total: not a euphemism but a substitution — "settlement" for colonisation, and a founding story in which the only problem being solved is British prison overcrowding. Germany's zero sits at the bottom of the ladder because it is the only one with a state's own contrary verdict standing outside the document: Berlin recognized the Herero and Nama genocide in 2021, and four years later the 460-question self-portrait still begins in 1933. A schoolbook might plead lack of space. A question bank that finds room for 160 questions about federal states' coats of arms has made a different decision.

And one grammatical possession is missing from all eight: not one abolition sentence in this corpus has a number with a currency sign in it. France counts the deported; Denmark counts the shipped; Britain counts the replacement workers. What nobody counts is what abolition cost and who was paid — a silence shared so evenly that, by this book's own rules, it is the genre speaking, not any one nation. What each nation chose is everything above.

4 Credit where due

The Livret du citoyen (FR) earns the credit line. It is the only document in the corpus that names an atrocity as a crime — with a number (1.2 million), a place the reader can visit (Nantes), a date the practice ended, and the law by which the state convicted itself (loi Taubira, 2001). It hands this to every applicant as required reading. The credit is paid in full before the Limits section notes what the same booklet does with 1802, and with Algeria.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

Genre does part of the work the ladder attributes to nations. The US and German entries are question banks; a bank has no room for narrative, and its zeros are cheaper than a booklet's. The comparison that survives the caveat: Spain's manual is also a question bank wrapped in prose and finds room for a history table; Germany's finds room for 160 regional-civics questions. The zeros are choices at the margin, not laws of the format.

France's credit stands next to its own silences: the 1794 first abolition is absent, so Napoleon's 1802 re-establishment arrives as one flat sentence with no baseline; the Algerian war "breaks out" with no belligerent named. The frankest page and the most evasive page are in the same booklet — this book's fairness rule cuts both ways.

Estonia and Austria are absent from this ladder because their absence is genre-normal, not avoidance: no Atlantic empire, nothing to omit. The Netherlands — a state that would owe this chapter a page — could not sit the exam: it publishes no book at all (ch. 11). And eight documents are not "the world": this slot's corpus is the corpus we hold.

Provenance: the UK quotes are from a re-typeset copy of the Crown-copyright text and the French quotes are transcribed from a scanned document; both are flagged for verification against the printed originals before this chapter is final.

7 The thread · the price of the portrait

Nine of the ten documents this book reads as peers can be downloaded free from the ministry that wrote them. The Danish ministry gives away 243 pages; the Instituto Cervantes gives away a manual with all three hundred questions and the answer key; Ottawa's booklet is free as a PDF and, on request, in print; the French Interior Ministry, the BAMF, the Australian Department of Home Affairs and USCIS all publish a file you can have in the next thirty seconds. The tenth is Life in the United Kingdom: A Guide for New Residents. It is Crown copyright — every page of our copy says so in the margin — and it has no free official download. It is published for the Home Office by TSO and sold at the official Life in the UK shop: the paperback £12.99, the eBook and audio edition from £12.99, the official practice questions from £7.99. The test itself is £50. (Latvia, outside the peer set, also sells a study book; it publishes no narrative test corpus and sits in chapter 11 as a silence.)

Nothing about that is scandalous on its own. Governments sell books; the Stationery Office sold Hansard for most of a century before its publishing arm was privatised, and TSO is its heir. What makes it this chapter's business is the coincidence of contents and price tag. Of the states that sit this slot's exam, Britain carried more people across the Atlantic than any other — the SlaveVoyages database estimates about 3.26 million embarked on British-flagged vessels, against 1.38 million on French and 111,000 on Danish. Its guide does not hide this. The paragraph opens with the frankest line any Anglophone booklet in the corpus gives the slot: "This commercial expansion and prosperity was sustained in part by the booming slave trade… dominated by Britain and the American colonies… Travelling on British ships in horrible conditions, they were taken to America and the Caribbean." British ships, British prosperity, named. It is what happens next that the ladder quoted — the Royal Navy as policeman, the two million "employed" — and the ladder should be read with the opening in view: the same two pages carry the frankest sentence and the smoothest one. That is this book's fairness rule, and Britain's guide illustrates it inside a single paragraph. Denmark's ledger is free. France's crime against humanity, with its number and its law, is free. Britain's version, frank opening and smooth ending together, is the one that comes with a receipt.

"There are currently (as of January 2013) two ways you can be tested on these requirements… Further details will be published on the UK Border Agency website."

UK · Life in the United Kingdom, 3rd ed. 2013, §1.2 "Becoming a permanent resident" · as it stands in our copy; current impression to be checked

The price buys an edition, and the edition has a date. The third edition went on sale in January 2013 and is still the current one; no fourth has appeared, and the Home Office's October 2025 promise to update the test has so far produced no new book. Later impressions have been amended at the margins — the passages on the late Queen, for one — but the edition number, the ISBN and the history chapters have not moved, and our copy still tells the applicant that "further details will be published on the UK Border Agency website," an agency whose abolition was announced two months after the book reached the shops. Frozen texts are not unique to Britain: Canada's booklet is a 2012 text in a 2021 reprint, and the free rows of the ladder include the oldest. But there is a difference between a frozen PDF and a frozen product. A book that is sold has a publisher, a stock, an ISBN, a Kindle edition, an impression count — and thirteen years of buyers who paid for the sentences this chapter has been reading, in the form they were fixed in the winter of 2013.

Which makes this the one row of the ladder that costs the reader money to check — and the only one that arrives with a receipt. The other nine can be downloaded and searched tonight; the tenth has to be bought, or borrowed from a public library, and it comes back as a paperback with a page number. Turn to chapter 3, "A global power," section "The slave trade." Every British sentence quoted in this chapter is on those pages, in this order: the booming trade and the British ships; the Quakers and Wilberforce; the Royal Navy stopping "slave ships from other countries"; the two million "employed"; and then the sentence this chapter has not quoted until now: they "worked on sugar plantations in the Caribbean, in mines in South Africa, on railways in East Africa and in the army in Kenya." The workplace, on the book's own account, did not change in 1833; the word for the workforce did. That is not an accusation the chapter is making — it is a sequence the guide prints, and has printed, at £12.99, to every applicant who has sat the test since 2013.

A provenance note that is also a finding. Our UK copy is a third-party re-typeset of the Crown text, and it is not pure 2013: someone has patched paragraphs into the politics chapter that run to the 2024 election ("The Labour party, lead by Keir Starmer, won the majority in the 2024 general election"), typo included, under the same "Crown copyright" running head. The free unofficial copy has been updated; the £12.99 official one is still the third edition. Every UK quote in this chapter is from the history chapter, where the re-typeset text matches the 2013 print as far as we can tell, and every one is flagged for eye-check against the TSO original before this chapter ships.

Now take your own country's test.
