Part III · The present tense · Chapter 9

Narrate vs test

On page 6 of the Australian booklet there is a page whose job is to be a border. It reads "The start of the testable section." Thirty-eight pages later another one reads "The end of the testable section." Between them is everything the state will examine; the pledge of loyalty the applicant will speak aloud at the ceremony is printed two pages before the border, outside the tested zone. No other document in the corpus draws the line as typography. Most of them do not draw it at all: Denmark writes seventy-one thousand words and never says, anywhere in two hundred and forty-three pages, what the exam covers.

The question · testability

Why does the state that narrates most test least — and which part of the exam does each state keep back?

1 What happened

  • The three longest narrators print almost no items: Denmark 70,974 words and none, Britain 37,357 words of Crown text and none, Canada 21,924 words (de-duplicated) and 27 study questions with no answers.
  • The three biggest banks narrate almost nothing: Germany 460 items and no prose at all, Spain 300 items inside 18,080 words of prose that never mentions the exam, the United States 100 items and their answers in 2,946 words.
  • Between them sit the two documents that do both, and they are the two that print the exam's rules: Austria (17,542 words, 27 keyed sample questions, the scoring table and the pass rule) and Estonia (19,494 words, 24 keyed samples, the pass mark, the time, the fee, the language).
  • Six of the ten core documents state at least the question count or the pass rule in the document itself. The four that state neither are the two banks that publish everything (Germany, Spain) and the two longest narratives (Denmark, France).
  • Word counts here are from the extractions and include running headers and captions — Denmark's header alone contributes some 730 words over 243 pages — so treat every figure as approximate to within a few per cent. Canada's extraction duplicates each spread, and all Canadian figures are halved.
  • Russia publishes, on the citizenship track, a demo paper and a specification with a codifier, a time limit and a threshold — the most complete set of exam rules in the corpus — and no narrative whatsoever. It sits in a box below, not on the ladder.

3 The tell

Read the verbs of disclosure in ladder order and the posture is audible: "are listed below" (the United States) → [the key printed, the format unspoken] (Spain) → "können … abweichen," may differ (Germany) → "finden Sie … mindestens eine Beispielfrage" (Austria) → "tests your knowledge of the following subjects" (Estonia) → "The start of the testable section" (Australia) → "are based on information provided in this study guide," "can be found" (Canada) → "based on ALL parts," "will not be enough" (Britain) → nothing at all (Denmark). The quantifiers do the same work: up to 10 of the 100, 6 out of 10 · 18 out of the 24 · at least 12 of 18 points · all five and 75 per cent overall · 24 questions on ALL parts · not fewer than 28 · and, in four documents, no number whatsoever.

The anti-correlation is real — the long books print few items, the banks print no prose — but stated that way it is a fact about page budgets, and the two documents in the middle disprove the causal version of it. Austria narrates seventeen thousand words and prints keyed samples with a scoring table. Estonia narrates nineteen thousand and prints a syllabus, a pass mark, a time and twenty-four keyed samples. So the interesting question is not how many items a narrator prints. It is which of three things each document withholds: the boundary (what is examinable), the answers (what the state counts as right), or the sampling rule (how the exam is drawn from the material). Britain withholds the answers and the sampling rule and declares the boundary to be everything. Canada withholds the answers and the sampling rule and prints its boundary as a statute. Australia prints the boundary as two pages and withholds everything else. Germany publishes the entire bank and withholds the answers. Spain publishes bank and answers and withholds the sampling rule. Denmark withholds all three. Estonia withholds the least: it publishes the boundary in four bullets, keys its samples, and tells the applicant plainly that a fresh paper is written for every sitting.

The last move is the filing decision — where the syllabus lives. Inside the book: Estonia's four subjects, Canada's Citizenship Regulations, Austria's three exam parts, Australia's start-and-end pages, Britain's "whole book," Russia's codifier inside its specification. Outside the book: Germany's on the agency's website, Spain's at the Instituto Cervantes, Denmark's at the ministry's testing portal, France's in a ministerial order of 10 October 2025. There is a pattern in that, and it is not the one you would guess: the states that publish the whole bank are the states that never say how it is sampled, and the states that publish no items at all are the ones that describe the room.

Which brings the chapter to the cut it exists for. Some material is narrated at length and never examined; some is examined and never narrated. Estonia's is the cleanest case in the corpus, because the exclusion is stated rather than inferred: ten pages on the occupations, with deportation numbers and dates, and a syllabus of four subjects that are all constitutional law. Austria narrates its acknowledgment of guilt — that citizens, firms and institutions of the state "Täter/innen waren" — and hangs no sample question on it, while the same chapter's two samples ask where the camp was and who was persecuted. Britain's forty per cent of history is declared wholly testable and shown not at all. And in the other direction: Germany's item 96 asks how a person who denies the Holocaust can be punished, offering "not at all, Holocaust denial is permitted" among the four options, in a document that contains no sentence about the Holocaust and no answer key. Spain asks in which museum you can see Guernica — the painting appears in the prose only as a picture caption, and the bombing is never narrated. Russia asks the applicant to write, in Russian, the significance of the battle in which the twenty-eight Panfilov heroes performed their feat, having never described that battle to them anywhere.

Sit this · the rules, and two items

The pass marks, side by side — the instruction the reader never read about their own citizenship: Austria, "in each of the three exam areas at least 3 points … or at least 12 points in total"; Australia, "all five of the values questions correctly, with a mark of at least 75 per cent overall"; Estonia, "at least 18 out of the 24"; the United States, "6 out of 10"; Russia, not fewer than 28 of 40 with at least 12 in each half, in 90 minutes. Germany, Spain and Denmark print no number at all. AT, Beispielfrage 8: "Welche für Österreich wichtigen Dinge passierten im Jahre 1955? Unterzeichnung des Staatsvertrages / Ende der Besatzungszeit / Ende des Zweiten Weltkrieges / Fall des Eisernen Vorhangs" — two are correct; tick "Ende des Zweiten Weltkrieges" and the item scores nothing, because a wrong tick zeroes it and "Raten zahlt sich nicht aus." DE, Aufgabe 96: "Wie kann jemand, der den Holocaust leugnet, bestraft werden? ▪ Kürzung sozialer Leistungen ▪ bis zu 100 Sozialstunden ▪ gar nicht, Holocaustleugnung ist erlaubt ▪ mit Freiheitsstrafe bis zu fünf Jahren oder mit Geldstrafe" — no key is published, so the reader has to decide for themselves which of those four is the state's answer.

4 Credit where due

Estonia's booklet, pages 63–64, handles this slot best in the corpus. It prints the number of questions, the pass mark, the time, the language, the fact that the exam is free, the right to have the Constitution and the Citizenship Act on the desk and to use a dictionary, a four-bullet syllabus, twenty-four samples with the answers marked, and the plain admission that a new paper is written for every sitting. By listing the four subjects it also does something no other narrator does: it tells the applicant which of its own pages will not be asked about. Being told is not being asked, and Estonia is the only document that says so out loud. Austria is the runner-up — a sample question with every chapter, the key in the back, the partial-credit rule, the pass rule, the retake rule, and the sentence that lowers the stakes of its own narrative: "Kein historisches Detailwissen." The American bank earns a technical credit for putting the whole universe of items and its pass mark on page 1, but it narrates nothing, so it does not handle this slot; it dissolves it.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

Genre carries the crude version of the finding: a bank cannot narrate and a booklet is not obliged to print items, so "long book, few questions" is partly a fact about what these documents are. What survives the baseline is narrower. Among the six narrators, only two print keyed samples (Austria, Estonia); only two mark a boundary between narrated and testable (Australia, Estonia); only one states nothing whatever about its exam (Denmark). Austria's "the third part is not in this book" is disclosure, not concealment. And the absence of a coverage statement is the only real signature here, because it belongs to one document out of ten.

The counts are honest but soft. Canada's extraction duplicates every spread, so all Canadian figures are halved, and its page numbers are printed folios covering two pages each. The Danish figure includes a running header worth about 730 words. The Australian "83 per cent testable" is a fact about the trimmed 2020 edition in this corpus, not about the full guide — the untested chapters were removed before the file reached us, so what can be seen outside the border is only front and back matter. Britain's word counts exclude the third-party summary pages of the re-typeset, and every British quote here — including the capitalised "ALL" — is provisional pending the eye-check against the TSO original. Germany's history-item count is a term-based estimate and is given as a range, some sixty to seventy of three hundred. Spain's item splitter recovers 259 of 300 items from a two-column layout, so item-level searches were confirmed against whole pages.

France is one line rather than a rung: the booklet has no text layer, and what is known of its exam comes from page-image transcriptions of p. 3 (a civic examination, and an interview to assess "votre degré d'assimilation à la communauté nationale") and p. 81, which points to Annexe I of the ministerial order of 10 October 2025 — so the syllabus is real and lives outside the book. Russia is boxed rather than ranked, and the two demo papers are not the bank: the specification's codifier is the syllabus, and the bank's size is published in neither file. The Netherlands and Latvia are the far end of this ladder and belong to ch. 11. Every pass mark and fee not printed in a document — Britain's, Canada's, Germany's, Spain's, Denmark's, France's — is an enrichment claim, marked for verification against the issuing agency, not a corpus fact.
