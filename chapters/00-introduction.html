The gradient

Seven questions. Each is a real item from a real state's naturalization exam, quoted from the document (option markers normalised here). Before reading on, decide which you could answer — and which country you hold a passport for without ever having been asked.

1 What this book reads

Before a state hands you its passport, it hands you a little book about itself and grades you on believing it. Sometimes the book is 243 pages long and never once addresses you. Sometimes it is 460 questions with no answers. Sometimes it is a hundred questions with the answers printed underneath, and the state adds that it "will not accept an incorrect answer." Sometimes it is a legal list of learning outcomes and no book at all, and sometimes — Japan, Israel, Belarus — there is nothing to read: in Japan and Israel admission is a minister's discretion, in Belarus no test material is published, and none of the three has written a portrait of itself for the occasion.

These documents are the shortest authorized self-portraits governments write, and the only history books whose readers sit an exam on them. That makes them unusually legible. A school textbook has three hundred pages to be balanced in; a citizenship guide has a fraction of that, and much of it is declared testable, so what a state leaves out, and what it makes an applicant affirm, carry more signal than the same choices would in a classroom. This book reads the official study materials of ten states against one another — the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, the United States, Germany, Austria, Denmark, France, Spain and Estonia — with Russia, the Netherlands and Latvia as special voices and Japan, Israel and Belarus as the zero column. Every claim ends in an edition and a page or question number.

2 The gradient

Sort the corpus by how much a state writes down before it will let you in, and a shape appears. At one end, a ministry textbook of 243 pages. At the other, nothing. In between, the states that narrate and the states that only ask — and they are not the same states.

Read the two middle columns together and the corpus's first finding is already visible: narration and examination anti-correlate. The two states that publish the most words (Denmark, Britain) print no scored items; the states that print the most items (Germany, Spain, the United States) narrate least or not at all. Estonia narrates ten pages of double occupation and sets twenty-four sample questions with no history in them. Germany sets seventy history items and publishes no answer key, so the document never exposes what the state's answer is. The most a state tells you and the most it asks you are, across the corpus, different documents.

3 The gradient is a gradient of trust

Length is one axis; the other is how much of the verdict the state lets you see in advance. Austria prints its sample questions, its answers, its scoring rules, a study calendar — and a sentence no other document has: "Die Subjektivität der Auswahl ist zwangsläufig," the subjectivity of the selection is unavoidable. The state tells the applicant its own history chapter is a choice. Estonia prints "Taking the examination is free of charge," the pass mark, the time allowed, and that a dictionary will be in the room. The United States prints all hundred answers and asks the applicant to use them even though "there may be additional correct answers." Germany prints 460 questions, no answers, and a warning that the wording may differ. Denmark prints 243 pages of syllabus and does not describe the exam. Russia's citizenship track publishes a demo whose open-answer key specifies which framing of Crimea will be marked correct. And past the end of the table, three states publish nothing and decide.

What a state publishes is how much of the judgement it is prepared to expose. This book treats that exposure as data — and it treats its own corpus the same way. The Russian document collected for this project was, on its own first page, the demo for a temporary residence permit, not for citizenship; the citizenship-track demo, published in August 2025, was found later and is what chapter 6 reads. Corrections of that kind are recorded in the Method rather than smoothed over, because a book about what states leave out cannot leave out what it got wrong.

4 How the book works

  • The unit is the slot, not the event. Guides do not share events; they share questions every state must answer or visibly decline — where does the country begin, what did it do in its empire, in the war, to the people who were already there, which chapter can it not ask about, what must you affirm, what wrong answers does it invent. Each chapter takes one slot and runs it across the corpus.
  • The ladder orders the passages by rhetorical posture — named-and-dated, qualified, agentless, filed elsewhere, absent — never alphabetically. Empty cells are meaningful and badged ("Stolen Generations: 0 mentions in the testable section"), with the search terms in the receipts.
  • Quotes or nothing. No claim about a document without the passage, original and translation, edition and page or item number. Distractors — the wrong answers a state writes — are quoted as text: a distractor is the state imagining error.
  • The baseline rule. An omission shared by most guides is the genre speaking, not a nation avoiding; a topic only one or two guides carry, or lack, is a signature. Question banks are graded against question banks.
  • Credit precedes critique. Every chapter names the document that handled its slot best. The same state is often the frankest narrator on one page and the most evasive on the next; the book shows both or neither.
  • The reader is in the chapter. You hold the passport these documents gatekeep and never sat the exam. Every chapter carries at least one real item you can try — and every chapter ends the same way.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

Fifteen positions are not the world; the corpus is what a state publishes in a form that can be read, and three states publish nothing readable. Editions are pinned and uneven — a 2012 Canadian text under a 2021 oath, a 2013 British handbook still on sale, a 2020 Australian edition trimmed to the testable pages, a French booklet that exists only as a scan, a Russian citizenship demo eleven months old. Two provenance caveats gate every British and French quotation: the UK text is a third-party re-typeset of the Crown-copyright original, and the French text is transcribed from page images; both are flagged for eye-check before any chapter is final. Word counts, page shares and item counts are from our extractions and are approximate to the page. And the gradient measures what is published, not what is asked in the room: Denmark's real questions and Germany's real answers are precisely the things the gradient cannot see.
