Part III · The present tense · Chapter 10

The invisible applicant

On page 194 of the Danish textbook there is a list a reader can look themselves up in: Turkey 68,000, Poland 57,000, Ukraine 50,000, Romania 46,000, Syria 46,000, Germany 43,000, Iraq 35,000, Lebanon 28,000, Iran 28,000, Pakistan 28,000. It is the top ten countries of origin of people living in Denmark, printed in the book the applicant will be examined on. It is the only place in this corpus where a person studying for a citizenship test can find their own country and its number. In the German catalogue the subject arrives as items 297 to 300 of 300 — after Easter customs and church tax. In the American bank it does not arrive at all.

The question · omission and people

These are books for immigrants. Does immigration appear in them as history — who came, when, why, what was done to them — or only as administration?

1 What happened

  • Six of the nine text documents narrate immigration as history: Denmark, Canada, Britain, Austria, Estonia, Australia. That is the genre norm, and the signatures are at the edges.
  • Two documents name an immigrant. Canada names several — Cabot "an Italian immigrant to England", Macdonald who "came to Upper Canada as a child", Strathcona "the Scottish-born director" of the railway, Corporal Filip Konowal "born in Ukraine", Phil Edwards "Born in British Guiana". Britain names three: Sake Dean Mahomet, Ludwig Guttman "a German refugee", Mo Farah "born in Somalia". The other seven documents have no named immigrant in them.
  • Two banks find room for the topic without a single date, name or group: Germany at four items out of 460, Spain as a residence card and a residence clock. The American bank has nothing: no immigrant, no migration, no Ellis Island, no refugee, no foreign, no born.
  • The noun each document uses for its reader varies more than anything else in this book: Neubürger/in and "neue Heimat" (Austria), "newcomers" (Canada), "your migration story" (Australia), "future citizens" and "applicant" (Estonia), "new residents" and "permanent migrant" (Britain), "the applicant" (the United States), extranjero (Spain), иностранный гражданин (Russia), udlænding in the third person (Denmark), and in Germany no noun for the applicant at all.
  • Two documents narrate discrimination against immigrants as something that happened: Denmark with its own statistics, and Canada with dated apologies. Two carry it as a hypothetical scenario (Austria's prose, Germany's item 281). The rest carry it, if at all, as a present-tense norm without an episode attached — Australia's "Racism has no place in Australia" and an address to report it to, and nothing that occurred.
  • One clause in these documents applies to naturalized citizens and not to people who were born citizens: the loss of the citizenship they are about to receive. Russia examines the applicant on it. Estonia states it and names the asymmetry. Denmark states it neutrally. Britain, Canada, Austria and Germany do not mention it.

2 The ladder

Ordered by how present the applicant's own kind is in the story: a section of its own with a census; builders with names and dated apologies; one dated section where the state does the recruiting; the applicant's own sentence in quotation marks; the arrival narrated as somebody else's alarming wave; one paragraph and a possessive; four items at the tail of a bank; a card and a waiting period; an examination on one's own revocability; nothing.

3 The tell

The verbs of arrival, read down the ladder: they came, settled, filled a need (Denmark) → immigrated, were turned away, fled, sought refuge (Canada) → were invited, were recruited, travelled to work and settle (Britain) → came, and were sent back by nobody (Austria) → was shipped in, toodi, was brought (Estonia) → had to leave, and our programs have brought (Australia) → were recruited by the Federal Republic (Germany, in a question stem) → apply for the card (Spain) → decided to become (Russia) → came, of colonists (the United States). The quantifiers sort the same way. Denmark: 977,000, 16.3 per cent, 21,000, 35,000, and a table with ten countries in it. Canada: 170,000, 115,000, 37,000, over 50,000. Britain: 28,000, 120,000, thirteen million who left, nearly ten per cent with a foreign-born parent or grandparent. Estonia: 72,000 to 240,000 to 475,000, and 61.5 per cent. Austria: "many", "a large number", and 120,000 who had to flee. Australia, Germany, Spain, the United States: no number at all.

Then the filing decision, which is where the verdict usually hides. Denmark files the applicant's history twice — a thematic spread at the back and a section inside the political chapter. Britain files it as a sub-section of "Britain since 1945". Austria splits it between the end of the monarchy and modern Austria. Estonia files it inside the occupation chapter, so that the arrival of the reader's likely community is an act of the Soviet Union rather than a chapter of Estonian social history. Germany files it at items 297 to 300. Spain files it under territorial organisation, as a question about which level of government has competence. The United States does not file it. And Russia files it under the foundations of legislation, as item 9 of the codifier: know the grounds for acquiring and terminating citizenship.

"Estonian citizenship obtained through birth cannot be taken away from anyone."

EE · What you need to know about the Republic of Estonia (2011), p. 59 — printed in the same paragraph that tells the applicant they must first renounce the citizenship they have. Estonia is the only document that writes the asymmetry down. Russia is the only one that examines it.

That is the finding this chapter exists for, and it takes the whole corpus to see. Every one of these documents could put to its reader the one clause that separates the two kinds of citizen — that a citizenship granted can be withdrawn, and a citizenship inherited cannot. Russia tests it, with a key. Estonia states it, and names the asymmetry in the same breath as the renunciation requirement. Denmark states it neutrally in the crime chapter: for very serious crimes such as terrorism a Danish citizen can be deprived of citizenship by court judgment, but not if that would leave them stateless — a proviso which in practice means the person with a second passport, which in practice means the naturalized. The United States has the applicant swear to "give up loyalty to other countries" and says nothing about the reverse. Australia's version is a side-disability: citizens "who are not dual citizens" may seek election to parliament. Spain mentions the subject once and only to deny a rumour. Britain, Canada, Austria and Germany do not raise it at all: no deprivation, no revocation, no Entzug, no Aberkennung. So the clause that would never apply to a reader born into their passport is also the clause most of these books decline to print — and the reader who has never sat one of these exams has probably never learned that this is what "by birth" buys.

One more seam, and it cuts against the obvious reading. The warmest words for the reader sit in the books that narrate the group: Austria's "new homeland" and Neubürgerin, Canada's "newcomers" and "you are helping to write the continuing story of Canada", Australia's "your migration story". The coldest sit in documents that administer the applicant or itemise them: Spain's extranjero, Russia's иностранный гражданин, Germany's silence — no Antragsteller, no Neubürger, no noun at all, only a "Sie" inside scenario questions. And then the exception that undoes the pattern: Denmark, which never addresses its reader in the second person once in 243 pages, files udlændinge in a paragraph about deportation, and carries the fullest account of the reader's own kind in the corpus. Warmth and candour are different axes.

Sit this · four real items and a clause

RU, Q24 (above; key 1 and 4): pick the two crimes for which a citizenship "obtained not by right of birth" is terminated. The reader's own citizenship is not on any such list. DE, Aufgabe 300: „Aus welchem Land kamen die ersten Gastarbeiterinnen und Gastarbeiter in die Bundesrepublik Deutschland? ▪ Italien ▪ Spanien ▪ Portugal ▪ Türkei" — no key is published. ES, item 2002 (key: Falso): must Spaniards naturalized by residence wait three years to vote? US, Q53: "What is one promise you make when you become a United States citizen?" — six accepted answers, the first of which is "give up loyalty to other countries." The reader never made that promise, and was never asked to. And the clause, EE p. 59: birth citizenship "cannot be taken away from anyone."

4 Credit where due

Denmark's textbook handles this slot best in the corpus, and by some distance. Three pages of the applicant's own history with dates, groups and numbers. The majority's hostility reported as opinion and attributed — "Mange danskere mente" — rather than smuggled in as fact. Its own tightening of the rules printed together with its critics, including the parenthesis that concedes the point applies to Danes too: the stricter family-reunification requirements have made it too hard, "også for danske statsborgere", to bring a spouse to the country. And then the discrimination spread, which no other document attempts: surveys suggesting that almost half of all citizens with a non-Western immigrant background have experienced discrimination; 3,107 recorded hate-crime cases in 2017–22, 1,709 of them on grounds of the victim's race or ethnicity; and a convicted party leader's words quoted verbatim, to mark where the legal line falls — that the Danes and Denmark are "far too highly developed and advanced to be able to coexist with the very low-standing Muslim cultural garbage." The state prints the sentence that was punished and does not name the man who said it. Canada is the runner-up: the only guide in which the immigrant is a proper noun, and in which excluded groups are named with a dated apology attached. Austria earns the honourable mention for one sentence — the future citizen, in quotation marks, saying who they are.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

Genre does real work at the bottom of the ladder: three banks and two demo papers have no room for narrative, so "administration only" is partly a format effect. What survives the baseline is narrower and sharper — Germany and Spain both find room for the subject and neither finds room for a date, a name or a group, and the American bank finds none at all. Six of nine text documents narrate immigration as history, so narrating it is the norm; the signatures are Denmark's three-page census with a named-country table at one edge, and the two banks' zero-as-history at the other. Named immigrants are a two-document signature (Canada, Britain), not a general practice.

The documents carry their frames into their credit. Canada's guide is the 2012 text, so its immigration numbers are pre-2012, and its one agentless sentence — the refugees "were turned away" in 1939 — sits two folios from two dated apologies. Denmark, which prints half its non-Western citizens' experience of discrimination, is also the document that never addresses its reader and files udlændinge beside deportation. Estonia's English and Estonian editions differ where it matters most: toodi, "was brought", becomes "was shipped in", and põhjendatult, justifiably, becomes "great" — a translator's decision, not the state's, and flagged as such. Australia's row is the trimmed 2020 edition: the two named migrants survive as bare website addresses because the trimming removed the pages they lived on, which is an editing artefact rather than an authorial choice. Every British quote here is provisional pending the eye-check against the TSO original; the re-typeset carries patched paragraphs elsewhere in the file.

France has no cell. The booklet is a scan with no text layer, and the pages that would matter for this slot — where it puts the loss of nationality, what its charter of rights and duties says — have no transcription on file, so nothing is claimed about them. Russia stays boxed: the 2023 paper is the temporary-residence exam and contains no migration history at all, and the 2025 citizenship demo's items 22 to 24 are the exhibit. The Netherlands and Latvia publish no book, so their treatment of this subject belongs to ch. 11. And the outside-corpus anchors that would sharpen several rows — the Danish party leader's name and case, the current Estonian rules on dual citizenship, the statutory list behind the Russian item, Britain's deprivation powers under the 1981 Act, Canada's post-2012 revocation politics — are all enrichment claims marked for verification, not corpus facts.
