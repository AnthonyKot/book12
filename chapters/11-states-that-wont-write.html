Part IV · The silence · Chapter 11

The states that won't write

The only free document the Latvian state hands a person who wants to become Latvian is four pages long. It was laid out in June 2021, co-funded by the European Union's asylum and migration fund, and it prints the fee — "Pamatnodeva 28,46 eiro" — the reduced fee, the six towns where the test is sat, every category of person excused from it, and the sentence that says who decides: "Lēmumu par uzņemšanu Latvijas pilsonībā naturalizācijas kārtībā pieņem Ministru kabinets." It also says the applicant must know "the basics of Latvian history and culture." It contains no date, no event and no name from Latvian history. The history is in a book. The same office sells it.

The question · testability and exposure

How much of the verdict does a state let the applicant see in advance — and when it publishes no book at all, what does it publish instead?

1 What happened

  • Five of the fifteen positions in this book have no state-authored study document: the Netherlands, Latvia, Japan, Israel and Belarus. Ten do. This chapter reads the five — and then turns the same question on the ten, because every one of them keeps some part of its exam unwritten.
  • The Netherlands fixes the content of its Kennis van de Nederlandse Maatschappij exam as law — the eindtermen, learning outcomes published in the Staatscourant, revised in 2024 for the exam introduced 1 July 2025 — and offers two browser practice exams; the textbooks are sold by commercial publishers.
  • Latvia's Office of Citizenship and Migration Affairs (PMLP) offers a browser self-test, a downloadable sample paper, a four-page leaflet, and a study book by J. Taurēns that it sells. The written test has 18 questions — ten on history and culture, eight on the Constitution — plus the anthem, written out or recited.
  • Japan's Ministry of Justice lists six statutory conditions for naturalisation and says that if the Minister permits it, the permission is announced in the Official Gazette; no test or study material is prescribed. Israel's Population and Immigration Authority lists residence, "some Hebrew," documents and declarations, and the Interior Minister's discretion.
  • Belarus: no published test corpus was found in the 2026-08-13 search; the ministry page it was checked against returned an empty page on 2026-08-17. The cell below says exactly that and no more.
  • Nothing here is a claim about what these five states require; it is a claim about what they publish. The Latvian leaflet was unreachable when the corpus was collected and was fetched for this chapter; that is recorded, not backdated.

2 The ladder

Fifteen positions ordered by how much of the verdict is exposed before the applicant sits down: the bank and its answers printed; the bank printed and the answers withheld; a sample and its key with the bank secret; a narrative and study questions; a narrative and nothing else; a paper published after the sitting; a syllabus and an interview; outcomes fixed as law with the book left to the market; a leaflet, a self-test and a book for sale; a statute and a permission; a page that no longer loads.

3 The tell

Read the verbs of exposure down the column. The United States: the answers "provided below." Spain: "Soluciones." Germany: the wording "may differ slightly" — können leicht abweichen — from the originals, which is the only sentence in the catalogue that admits an original exists. Austria: "you will find at least one sample question," and, for a third of the exam, "you learn … on your Land's website." Estonia: "A new examination is produced every time." Canada: "similar to the questions." Australia: "randomly selected." Britain: "different for each person." Denmark: no verb — a running header. France: "you will be invited to an interview." The Netherlands: vastgesteld, fixed, in a government gazette. Latvia: "try the tool," and "the Cabinet of Ministers takes the decision." Japan: "permits." Belarus: an empty page.

Now the numbers. Printed items: 100 · 300 · 460 · 27 · 24 · about 27 · 0 · 0 · 0 · 0 · 0 · 0 · 0 · 0 · 0. But the number of items printed is not the number public. Denmark's 45 become public one sitting late; Germany's 460 are public and their answers are not; the Dutch exam is public as law and private as a book; Latvia's is a sample paper, a self-test and a book with a price. The baseline rule cuts hard here: every core state hides something, so "keeps part of the exam unwritten" is genre norm, not a national trait. What is signature is which half. Germany alone publishes a bank and withholds its key. Denmark alone tests ten items no book can contain and publishes the paper afterwards. The Netherlands alone makes the self-portrait a market product by design. Latvia alone gives away the fee and sells the history.

"Lēmumu par uzņemšanu Latvijas pilsonībā naturalizācijas kārtībā pieņem Ministru kabinets."

LV · PMLP leaflet, 2021 — "The decision on admission to Latvian citizenship by naturalisation is taken by the Cabinet of Ministers." One of the leaflet's few complete sentences about the state; the only one with a subject.

The filing decision is the last tell: where a state puts the applicant's exam. In a booklet (Austria, Estonia, Australia, Britain, Canada); on page 1 of the bank (the United States); on a website (Germany, Spain, Denmark); in a ministerial order (France); in the Staatscourant (the Netherlands); in a leaflet that names the fee and the Cabinet (Latvia); in a permission notice in the Official Gazette (Japan). The last three never say "history" with a date attached. That is the narrow claim of this chapter — not that the five bookless states are silent about their past (the Netherlands fixes history outcomes in law; Latvia sells a history book), but that no state-authored self-portrait exists for the applicant to be graded on. What is graded instead is a person's file, and the sentence that says so has a subject: a Cabinet, a Minister.

Sit this · three real lines

LV, leaflet: "jānorunā vai jāuzraksta valsts himna" — write out your own national anthem now, from memory, on paper; each part is graded separately. DE, Aufgabe 218: „Wie viele Bundesländer kamen bei der Wiedervereinigung 1990 zur Bundesrepublik Deutschland hinzu? ▪ 4 ▪ 5 ▪ 6 ▪ 7" — pick one; no key is published, and the catalogue warns the wording may differ from the original. UK, p. 7: "The 24 questions will be different for each person taking the test at that test session" — the reader cannot rehearse the paper; nobody can.

4 Credit where due

Estonia's guide, page 64, handles this slot best in the corpus: it prints twenty-four sample questions with the key, tells the applicant plainly that "a new examination is produced every time," and then describes the room — "at least 18 out of the 24," 45 minutes, "free of charge," the law texts and a dictionary on the desk, a free consultation beforehand. The state that publishes the least about itself (a 2010 booklet, still current) tells the applicant the most about what will happen to them. Austria is the runner-up, for admitting that its samples are samples, that the selection is a sample evaluation without claim to completeness, and that a third of the exam lives elsewhere. And a small installment to Latvia: the leaflet lists every exemption and every reduced-fee category and prints "28,46 eiro" — no document in the core corpus prints a fee.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

Genre carries the top of the ladder: the German, Spanish and American rows are question banks, and a bank has no room for "we" — the exposure ladder measures machinery, not sentiment. Denmark's silence about its own exam is a signature only because the book is 243 pages of syllabus with a running header naming the exam on every one of them; the 35-of-45 split and the hosted past papers are the ministry's description, not the book's, and are marked for a check. The Dutch, Latvian and Japanese cells rest on official web pages read on 2026-08-17 and on the collection manifests of 2026-08-13; the Israeli cell was not re-read; the Belarusian source page returned nothing, and the cell says so rather than filling the gap with a word like "discretion" the manifest never used.

The Latvian leaflet is a 2021 file that the collection manifest recorded as unreachable and that arrived for this chapter; whether it is still linked from the naturalisation page, and whether €28.46 is still the fee, are enrichment claims to check, not corpus facts. Britain's row is from the re-typeset and awaits the TSO eye-check; France's from page-image transcriptions; Canada's folios are the print's, with the extraction's duplicated spreads discounted. And a side-finding surfaced while searching the Australian file: page 28 reads "His Majesty King Charles III" under a 2020 copyright line — the "2020 testable edition" was patched after September 2022, and the corpus list needs to say so. Russia (special voice) publishes on its citizenship track the most machinery in the corpus — a demo, a specification, a codifier, marking criteria, a pass mark of 28 of 40 with at least 12 in each half, 90 minutes — and no narrative; it is boxed in ch. 6 and the epilogue, not ranked here.
