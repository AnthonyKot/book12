Part II · The past · Chapter 5

First peoples

The Australian citizenship booklet's first mention of the people who were already there is on the imprint page, before the contents: "Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander readers are warned that this publication contains images and/or content relating to deceased persons." In the fifty pages that follow, no deceased Aboriginal person is named. The Canadian booklet's first mention is on page 2, in the oath every applicant swears aloud: "the Aboriginal and treaty rights of First Nations, Inuit and Métis peoples." The American question bank's is item 59 of 100: "Who lived in America before the Europeans arrived?" This chapter counts the pages, and reads the verb that joins the people who were there to the people who came.

The question · space and agency

How many pages before the first person who was already there is named — and what verb joins them to the newcomers?

1 What happened

  • Three of the ten core documents belong to settler states (CA, AU, US); one belongs to a state with an Arctic former colony inside its realm (DK, Greenland). The six European guides are the baseline: none has a settler slot, and their silence is genre — except where they narrate their own empires (ch. 3) and name nobody in them.
  • Two states in this chapter apologised in the same year: Australia's National Apology to the Stolen Generations (13 February 2008) and Canada's apology for residential schools (11 June 2008). One apology is a sentence in the testable body of a guide; the other is a web address at the bottom of a list.
  • Editions matter here: Canada's text is 2012 (before the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's 2015 report; its word is "Aboriginal", not "Indigenous") under a 2021 oath; Australia's is the 2020 "testable section", from which the non-examinable history was cut. Both effects are named in the Limits and not counted against the states.
  • The American bank prints a list of twenty-two tribe names as acceptable answers and tells the examining officer they "will be supplied with a list of federally recognized American Indian tribes."

2 The ladder

Seven self-portraits, ordered by posture: a named policy with a dated apology; a ledger with an independence clause and a passive relocation; presence without contact, with the apology as a URL; a question bank's "before" and a list, with nothing between; land without people; discovery grammar; zero. Read the verb.

3 The tell

Count the pages first. Canada names the people who were there on page 2, in a line the applicant will say out loud; the 2012 body catches up on page 10, with a column headed "Aboriginal peoples". Australia's first mention is a warning about the dead, before the contents; the first inhabitants get a paragraph on page 8, and the apology is a link on page 46, last on a list under "Other", after Public Holidays. The American bank reaches them at item 59, one after the colonists' six motives and one before the enslaved. Denmark reaches Greenlanders on page 18, as a population to be made Christian and sold sealskin. Britain, Spain and Germany never do — Britain maps a coast, gains control of a country, establishes colonies; Spain discovers a continent; Germany asks nothing.

"…hvorved bopladsen for flere grønlandske familier blev flyttet for at give plads til basen."

DK · Læremateriale, p. 198 · "whereby the settlement of several Greenlandic families was moved to make room for the base" — the Americans build; the settlement is moved by no one named; Denmark is not in the sentence

Now the verbs that join the two peoples. Canada's are the frankest in the corpus and the most revealing when read in sequence: the federal government "placed" children in schools "to educate and assimilate them" — the purpose in the state's own words — the schools "inflicted hardship", "some were physically abused", "Ottawa formally apologized"; the Métis "were not consulted"; the NWMP was founded "to pacify the West"; and Aboriginal people "were granted the vote" by nobody in particular. Denmark's are a ledger with one blank: the Inuit "indvandrede" — immigrated, the same verb the book uses for the applicant — around 1200, while the Norse were there "allerede", already, in the 900s; Greenland "was a colony"; and the Greenlanders "are a people in their own right with title to full independence" — a state telling its applicants that part of the realm may leave. The blank is Thule: the one paragraph in eight pages where Denmark did something to Greenlanders has no Danish subject, no number, no date beyond a decade, and, in 243 pages, no undskyld. Australia has no joining verb at all. Its two peoples occupy adjacent paragraphs — 65,000 years, then eleven convict ships — and no sentence in the testable section contains a settler and an Aboriginal person as co-actors; the survivor of the founding paragraph is "the colony". Its passives are exact: Aboriginal people "were included in official estimates" after 1967, and who had excluded them (section 127 of the Constitution) is never in the sentence, in three tellings. The American verb is "lived… before", and the bank's other verb for the continent is "buy from France".

Then the sharpest single contrast in the corpus, and it is between two Commonwealth states in one year. In 2008 Canada's prime minister apologised for residential schools and Australia's for the Stolen Generations. Canada's guide carries it as a sentence in the testable body: "In 2008, Ottawa formally apologized to the former students." Australia's carries it as a URL in the back matter of an edition from which the passage the link once supported was cut — the list is introduced as websites for organisations "referred to in the resource book", and the referring pages are gone. Inside the fifty testable pages: Stolen Generations, zero; Sorry, zero; Mabo, native title, terra nullius, massacre, frontier, all zero. The trigger warning on the imprint page survives the removal of everything it warned about. The Spanish manual makes the same filing move in a smaller room: 12 October is examined as a date (item 5033) and its reason is nowhere; Columbus's arrival exists in the question bank only as a wrong answer to what 6 December celebrates.

And a mirror, for the Limits: the only European guide that uses the vocabulary of indigeneity uses it about the majority. Estonia's booklet writes that "the proportion of the indigenous population had shrunk to just 61.5 percent, which was a cause of great concern to the Estonians." The Estonian-language edition says the concern was põhjendatult — justified. The English edition, the one the applicant from that other 38.5 percent will read, drops the adverb.

Sit this · three real items

US, Q87: "Name one American Indian tribe in the United States." Try to name one, then a second, before looking at the list above — the officer holds the federal register. CA, the oath: "…that I will faithfully observe the laws of Canada, including the Constitution, which recognizes and affirms the Aboriginal and treaty rights of First Nations, Inuit and Métis peoples" — ten lines every new Canadian says aloud and no born Canadian ever has. ES, item 5033: "La Fiesta Nacional de España es el… a. 6 de diciembre. b. 15 de agosto. c. 12 de octubre." — and then, from memory, why.

4 Credit where due

Canada's booklet earns the credit for this slot: it is the only guide in the corpus that names a state policy, its agent, its purpose in the state's own words, a harm and a dated apology in one paragraph; that tells the applicant "treaties that were not always fully respected" and "the 12,000 Métis of the Red River were not consulted"; that prints "Métis Resistance" as a caption and calls a man it records as executed for high treason "seen by many as a hero"; and whose oath makes every new citizen affirm Aboriginal and treaty rights before anything else. The installment is paid alongside: the same book writes "some were physically abused" and "were granted the vote". Denmark takes the runner-up line for "a people in their own right with title to full independence" and for printing, two pages later, a Greenlandic critic of Denmark by name; Australia a line for weighing archaeology and creation stories in one sentence, joined by "however", and for putting "the traditional owners of the land in your area" into the ceremony itself; the United States a line for being the only question bank that names the first peoples at all.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

Two edition effects are doing work in this ladder and are not counted against the states. Canada's text is 2012: the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's report and the vocabulary shift to "Indigenous" postdate it, so those zeros are the calendar's; the Indian Act zero holds regardless — the statute that made "reserve land" and "status Indian" is absent while both terms are used. Australia's is the 2020 trimmed edition, from which the non-testable history was removed; the URL cell depends on that trim, and the full resource book should be read before claiming what was cut. What remains a choice is which pages were made testable, and the trigger warning that stayed.

Genre grades the banks: a zero in Germany's or Spain's catalogue is baseline unless another bank carries the item, and the American bank carrying two makes those zeros legible — Spain's more so, since its own timeline has America and 1492 in it and no people. The American list itself is a text: twenty-two names in no discernible order, including "Arawak" and "Inuit", which the officer's federal register may not contain. France is a gap, not a zero: the booklet has no text layer and nothing on the Kanak, Polynesian or Guianese peoples or the Nouméa Accord was recorded in the corpus recon; a page-image check is owed before France is filed either way. Austria and Estonia have no settler slot; their "already there" are domestic minorities, out of scope here (Estonia's inversion is in the tell). The Thule figures — the number of families, the 1999 court ruling — are outside the corpus and unstated.
