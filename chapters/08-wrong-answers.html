Part III · The present tense · Chapter 8

The wrong answers

Item 267 of the German naturalization catalogue describes a 22-year-old woman living with a boyfriend her parents dislike, and asks what the parents can do. Three of the four options are: they have the right to fetch their adult daughter back to the parental home; they can go to the police and report her; they can find another man for her. Somebody wrote those sentences. Somebody in a federal office typed out a power that does not exist in German law, in a document handed to people applying to live under it. This chapter reads the wrong answers — the only part of these documents that a state has to invent.

The question · distractors

What does a state write when it has to make up an error?

1 What happened

  • Six of the eleven documents print wrong answers: Germany's 460-item catalogue (no key), Spain's 300-item CCSE bank (key printed), Austria's 27 sample questions (key printed, penalty scoring), Estonia's 24 samples (starred key), Russia's two demo papers (keys printed), and Canada's three multiple-choice study questions (no key).
  • The United States prints a bank with no wrong answers at all: 100 questions with their acceptable answers, and the instruction "The USCIS Officer will not accept an incorrect answer."
  • Britain, Australia, Denmark and France print no items, right or wrong. Both the British handbook and the Australian book describe a multiple-choice test and show none of it. Four of ten core states therefore have no distractors by format, not by choice — the baseline this chapter is read against.
  • Germany publishes no answer key anywhere in its 191 pages, and warns on page 1 that "the wording of individual questions and answers may differ slightly from the original questions used." Where an answer is named below it is because it is a matter of public fact, and it is said to be.
  • Austria prices the wrong answer: four options of which "at least one but at most three are correct," and a wrong tick scores nothing for the whole item. The margin note reads "Raten zahlt sich nicht aus" — guessing does not pay.
  • Estonia's exam is open-book: the applicant may consult the Constitution and the Citizenship Act in Estonian and use a dictionary. Its distractors are written to survive a reader holding the statute.

2 The ladder

Ordered by what the state does with the error it invents: writes out the belief it wants gone; borrows a neighbour's true fact; offers a right it does not grant; offers a world worse than the one it runs; offers the applicant their own status; refuses a synonym; shows no wrong answer at all; prints no answer at all.

3 The tell

Start with the object of the refusal, because it is different in every document. Austria refuses a tick and prices it: nothing for the item, and "guessing does not pay." Estonia refuses an option and marks the survivor with a star. Spain refuses a whole sentence — Falso is a verdict on a proposition, not a choice between nouns. Russia's citizenship demo refuses a synonym, and does it in the marking criteria where the applicant cannot see it while answering. The United States refuses without showing: "will not accept an incorrect answer," and no incorrect answer is ever printed. Germany prints four hundred and sixty questions with their errors fully written out and never says which was which. Down the ladder, what shrinks is not the number of wrong answers but how much of its own fear the state is willing to put on paper.

Then the negative stem, which is the sharpest instrument in the corpus. Some seventeen of Germany's three hundred general items ask what is not the case — "Was ist kein …", "Was steht nicht im Grundgesetz", "Welche Aufgabe gehört nicht dazu" — and sixteen more, one per Land, ask which minister a Land does not have. In every one of these the three wrong options are true facts about Germany and the "answer" is the invented sentence. Item 159 does it with the Nazi state: "What did Germany not have during the period of National Socialism?" offers free elections, press censorship, arbitrary arrests, and the persecution of Jews. Three of those are what the state did; the applicant's task is to find the one it did not do. The inverted item is the most efficient self-portrait in the corpus, and nobody designed it to be one.

„An demokratischen Wahlen in Deutschland teilzunehmen ist …“ — „eine Pflicht.“ / „ein Recht.“ / „ein Zwang.“ / „eine Last.“

DE · Gesamtfragenkatalog, Aufgabe 114 — "Taking part in democratic elections in Germany is … a duty / a right / a compulsion / a burden." Germany prints compulsory voting as a wrong answer four times. Australia prints it as law: "Voting is compulsory in Australia."

That is the hinge the comparison found, and it only exists because the documents are read side by side: one state's law is another state's wrong answer. Germany writes the duty to vote as an error in items 5, 114, 115 and 125; Spain offers "un derecho / un deber / una obligación" and keys the right; Australia's book says "Voting is compulsory in Australia. This shows the importance of participating in elections," and warns of a fine. Germany's item 22 offers "Monarchie" as a wrong form of state, in a corpus where five of the ten narrators are monarchies. Item 44 asks which office citizens cannot elect directly and answers with the presidency — the office Austrians elect directly. Item 94 puts the voting age at "16 / 18 / 21 / 23" and treats sixteen as wrong, while the same applicant's Bremen paper asks from what age one may vote for the Bürgerschaft and offers "14 / 16 / 18 / 20", and Austria's sample question 15 keys "men and women from 16." The distractor is not a lie. It is a fact from somewhere else, or from an earlier version of home: the Internationale and the DDR anthem in item 40, two DDR decorations in item 102, three franchises Germany really had in item 120, "Volkskammer" in item 74. The second German state survives in this document chiefly as an incorrect option.

Spain files its own past the same way, and more quietly. Its false sentences are pre-1978 law with the date taken off: a constitution obliging religious practice, exit visas refused on ideological grounds, police entering houses without a warrant, judges taking instructions from the government. The word "Franco" appears in a single item of three hundred. The dictatorship is not narrated here; it is the wrong answer. And Spain has the corpus's other specialty, the too-bad-to-be-true distractor — free health care only for the over-65s, social security for everyone except the unemployed — where the invented world is meaner than the real one. Germany's errors are mostly worlds with fewer rights; a few, like the holidays and the free newspapers, are worlds with more.

Sit this · four real items, four plausible mistakes

DE, Aufgabe 114 (above): an Australian reader picks "eine Pflicht" and is right at home and wrong in Berlin. No key is published. AT, Beispielfrage 27: tick the EU rights — and if you tick "Sich die Staatsbürgerschaft eines EU-Landes auszusuchen," the whole item scores zero (key p. 82: two marks). ES, item 2002 (p. 33, key Falso): "Los españoles que obtienen la nacionalidad por residencia deben esperar tres años para poder votar en las elecciones" — a false sentence about the applicant's own future, which the reader who never naturalised has no way to judge. EE, sample Q22: "Who decides whether to grant citizenship?" — most readers pick the board with "Citizenship" in its name; the key is the Government of the Republic.

4 Credit where due

Spain's CCSE manual handles this slot best: it is the only document that prints its wrong answers and its key, in a book that also narrates, and its false sentences do the work of a history chapter without a sneer — the state saying what it is no longer, one proposition at a time. It also prints an unflattering true answer where two flattering ones were available: "¿Cuántas mujeres han sido presidentas de Gobierno en España? a. Ninguna. b. Una. c. Dos." The key is a: none. Austria is the runner-up for showing its work — the scoring rules, the worked example with Moscow and New York in it, and the key in the back — which is why its invented province can be spotted at all. Germany's refusal to publish a key is a limit on what can be said about the richest set of distractors in the corpus, and it is noted as such below rather than treated as a fault.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

Distractors need a question bank, so the four core states that print none — Britain, Australia, Denmark, France — are a format fact, not an evasion; their wrong answers exist and are secret. The typology above is built on six documents and does not generalise to "states." Germany publishes no key, so every sentence about it here is of the form "the catalogue offers X beside Y"; where an answer is named it is a matter of public fact (that the rule of law binds the state, that torture is not compatible with the Basic Law, that the anthem begins "Einigkeit und Recht und Freiheit"). Page 1 of the catalogue also warns that the printed wording may differ from the live test's, which means the distractors read here are the state's approximation of its own distractors.

The Spanish extraction is two-column, and forty-six of three hundred items have their number detached from their text; those are cited by page with the detachment noted, and need an eye-check before ship — including the compulsory-voting item on p. 24, whose number the key resolves as 1085. Austria's key marks float above their option lists in the extraction: the counts of marks are reliable, their positions are not, so only counts are stated unless the answer is a matter of public fact. Canada's extraction duplicates the spread carrying the study questions and interleaves the option sets with the open questions. Russia is a special voice on both tracks — the 2023 paper is the temporary-residence exam and is labelled so at every use, and the 2025 citizenship demo is one variant of a bank this book does not hold. Britain is from the re-typeset and awaits the TSO eye-check; France has no text layer, so nothing is cited from it.

Two freshness notes. The German catalogue is dated 07.05.2025 and asks who the current Chancellor is, offering Schröder, Merkel, von der Leyen and Merz — the day after the Bundestag elected Merz. Item 73 asks for the two largest Bundestag groups and offers "CDU/CSU und AfD" first; on the 2025 seat counts that is the factually true option, and with no key printed the catalogue never says so. And the American 2025 revision — 128 questions, twenty asked, twelve to pass — is not in this corpus; nothing is said here about its wrong answers, and it prints answers only in any case.
