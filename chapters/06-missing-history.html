Part II · The past · Chapter 6

The missing history

The Russian demonstration exam is eighteen pages long and has seven history questions. They run: Prince Vladimir baptises Rus'; Moscow is liberated from the Poles in 1612; the Patriotic War of 1812 ends in Russian victory; 1917, a revolution; 1922, the USSR; 1993, the Constitution; Crimea "became part of Russia" under which president. The year 1945 appears once in the document — as the first, wrong option to the question of when the current Constitution was adopted. This chapter is about the page where a chapter should be, and what each state writes on it instead.

The question · omission and filing

Which chapter of its own past can a state not ask an applicant about — and what does it write on the page where that chapter would be?

1 What happened

  • This chapter runs the same test on every document: for each state, the three to five episodes any school history would carry as its worst — and a search, term by term, both languages, for whether the guide names them, renames them, or leaves the page blank. Every zero below lists its search terms in the receipts.
  • Three anchor cases were named before the search began: Russia without the Great Patriotic War, the United States without Jim Crow, Spain with Franco "isolated". All three held. The rest of the corpus was then checked the same way.
  • The baseline rule from the Method governs the verdicts: an omission shared by most guides is the genre speaking; a topic only one or two guides carry, or only one or two lack, is a signature. Chapters 3–5 have already paid out slavery, the war and first peoples; here those rows are cross-referenced, not re-laddered.
  • Russia's document is a demonstration variant with seven history items, not the approved bank; the claim is about what the published demo chooses, never "Russia never tests the war". It sits below as a boxed exhibit, not a rung.

2 The ladder

Nine documents, ordered by how the gap is handled: named with a date and an adverb of regret; a full ledger with no word for apology; the event without the name; numbers up to the border of the local hand; body counts without hands; the crime as a legal category and the act as a wrong answer; the positive half only; filed under literature; the movement without the thing. Then the boxed exhibit.

3 The tell

Each rung has a substitute grammar, and reading them in order is the chapter. Canada's is an adverb: "Regrettably," twice, each time with an "even though" that says the state was warned. Denmark's is the ledger passive — sentences "blev gennemført", a settlement "blev flyttet" — the fullest account of a state's own wrongs in the corpus, down to two ministers convicted for what they did to refugees and asylum-seeking couples, and no word for apology in 243 pages. Austria's is the indefinite subject: "manche Bürger/innen… Täter/innen waren", in a decade with no occasion — the 1980s "began to talk", and the name every Austrian would supply for why is not there. Estonia's is the transitive verb that stops: 10,000 Jewish prisoners "toodi", were brought, and the sentence ends. Britain's is weather and the intransitive plural: "the potato crop failed"; "many died… from disease"; "3,000 people lost their lives." Germany's is the legal category and the distractor: the catalogue can name "the National Socialist crimes against Jews" as the ground of a foreign policy, and can punish denial, and has no item in which Germans do a thing to anyone between 1939 and 1945 — the one sentence in which they do is a wrong answer. Australia's is the complement: "programs to actively encourage British migrants", which is the White Australia policy with its other half removed. Spain's is a change of discipline. And America's is a movement with its object deleted.

"What movement tried to end racial discrimination? ▪ civil rights (movement)"

US · Civics Questions, Q84 · the discrimination has no author, no law, no region and no date; the bank's other race item, Q48, files the poll tax as a thing you "don't have to pay"

Filing decisions are verdicts, and this slot is where they show most. Spain's history table gives Franco one row and one verb — "está aislada", Spain is isolated, the state as the patient of the world's rejection, with the dictatorship's domestic record carried by three adjectives. The manual's four occurrences of "memoria histórica" are all in the culture section: novelists and a film director. Its own phrase for what the table leaves out — "historias silenciadas de la posguerra", silenced stories of the postwar — is attributed to Almudena Grandes. The exam meets the dictatorship as a novel's subtitle (item 4004, Nada, "a novel about the Spanish postwar") and as a museum ticket (item 4026: in which museum can you see Guernica). The only item containing the word Franco asks about a king. Estonia files its one live genocide case — the 1949 deportations — in the law chapter, under retroactivity, not the history chapter. Denmark files the Tamil case under "Rigsretten", constitutional machinery, and Thule under a Greenland box. Australia files the apology under "For more information → Other". Germany files item 96, Holocaust denial, between tax and voting.

Now the baseline rule, applied honestly. An own-dictatorship-or-occupation row exists in every narrator that had one — Spain, Germany, Austria, Estonia, Denmark — so its mere presence is genre; the verb is the finding. But a twentieth-century race-exclusion law is named by Canada (the Head Tax) and by Britain ("new laws to restrict immigration… a strong connection to Britain through birth or ancestry") and by neither Australia (White Australia, 0) nor the United States (Jim Crow, 0): two settler democracies whose exclusion laws are present only as their remedies — a two-guide signature, not a genre effect. And post-1945 domestic self-critique is carried by Denmark (convicted ministers) and Austria (perpetrators, restitution) and by Germany not at all: RAF, NSU, Solingen, Rostock, Hanau, zero — a signature, because two neighbours show the genre can carry it. Only one document omits the event its own state calendar centres on, and that is the Russian demo — on seven items, which is why it is boxed and not ranked.

4 Credit where due

Denmark's textbook takes the credit for this slot: it is the only guide in the corpus that prints its own collaboration policy by name and gives it a cost-benefit, prints the postwar death sentences with the count carried out, and prints two ministers convicted for what they did to refugees and asylum couples — wrongs against the very people the book is addressed to. Canada, with three dated apologies and two "Regrettably"s, is the runner-up here only because its atonement grammar has already been paid out in chapters 3 and 5. The credit stands before the Limits note that Denmark's two Greenland wrongs apologised for since 2020 are absent, and that undskyld never appears.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

Banks are cheap places for zeros: the American, German and Russian documents cannot narrate, and their silences cost less than a booklet's. The comparators are inside the genre — Spain's bank finds room for a Franco row and Germany's for item 288 — so the American zero on segregation is a choice at the margin, and the finding is scaled to that. Australia's is the trimmed testable section; its zeros may live in the untested chapters of the full book, and "testable section: 0" is the exact claim. Russia's is a demo of seven items; the full approved bank is not in our hands, and nothing here says what it contains.

Denmark's credit stands next to a dated silence: the prime minister's apology for the 1951 Greenlandic children and the state's response on the IUD campaign both fall close to this edition's August 2025 print date, and the material carries neither. The Estonian "no local hand" reading is corpus-internal; the historical record of local participation is outside it and unstated. Britain's Bloody Sunday, Amritsar and Windrush zeros are dated by later apologies and reviews the 2013 text could not carry only for the last. The Waldheim reading is a null, not an overclaim: Austria's booklet never uses "Opfer", so the "first victim" thesis is neither stated nor refuted in it. France is cited only from the corpus recon ("deux conflits meurtriers éclatent" with no belligerent; colonial torture, harkis, 17 October 1961, Sétif not recorded) — no zeros are printed for a document with no text layer. The states that would owe this chapter their own paragraph — the Netherlands, Latvia, Japan — did not write the book (ch. 11).
