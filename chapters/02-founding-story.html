Part II · The past · Chapter 2

The founding story

On a runestone at Jelling, raised in the tenth century, a king records that he "won for himself all Denmark and Norway and made the Danes Christian." The Danish citizenship textbook reproduces the sentence on page 9 and tells the applicant the stone "is therefore often called Denmark's baptism certificate." Nine hundred kilometres south, the German naturalization catalogue — 460 questions, no prose — contains no correct answer dated before 1933. Both documents are the state's account of where it comes from, handed to the person about to join it. This chapter reads ten of them at their first sentence.

The question · space and people

Where does the state's history begin — and who is standing there when it does?

1 What happened

  • Every state in this chapter has at least two foundings: an old one it narrates (a runestone, a charter, a landing, an inscription) and a modern document it tests — Confederation 1867, Federation 1901, the Declaration of 1776 and Constitution of 1787, the Grundgesetz of 1949 and unification of 1990, the Staatsvertrag of 1955, the Constitution of 1978, the declaration of 1918 and its restoration in 1991, Grundloven of 1849.
  • Seven of the ten documents narrate; three (US, DE, and the question-bank half of ES) can only ask. For a bank, "where history begins" means: the earliest year that is a correct answer.
  • Two states in the corpus have citizenship laws in which the founding date is itself operative: Estonia's 16 June 1940 cut-off (below) and, outside the ladder, the Federal Republic's Article 116. Only Estonia's guide says so to the applicant.
  • National days in the corpus: 1 July (CA), 26 January (AU), 4 July (US), 3 October (DE), 26 October (AT), 5 June (DK, Grundlovsdag), 24 February (EE), 12 October (ES), 14 July (FR). Each is a choice among the state's foundings; the applicant's ceremony usually lands on it.

2 The ladder

Ten founding sentences, ordered by posture: from the state that says it was always here, through the state that says it came back, the state that says everyone arrived, the states that begin when a name is written down, to the states whose founding is a question with four options — and, last, the one guide that admits its founding year is disputed. Watch who is in the sentence.

3 The tell

Start with a fact the ladder makes visible only when the rows sit together: every narrating guide begins its history at the moment the country's name is first written down by somebody else. Britain's history "begins," on the guide's own criterion, when kings' names appear on coins. Denmark's begins when "Danmark" is cut into a runestone. Austria's name arrives "in the charter of a German emperor" in 996 — and, on the following pages, is fixed a second time by the victors of 1919, who "also laid down the name of the state: 'Republik Österreich'." Canada's name is a word two "captured guides" say to Cartier. Spain's 1492 row files Columbus, Granada and the first Castilian grammar in one line, as if the language and the empire were the same event. The founding, in the narrating guides, is a naming — and the namer is usually a conqueror, an emperor, or a treaty.

"European settlement started when the first 11 convict ships… arrived from Great Britain on 26 January 1788."

AU · Our Common Bond, testable ed. 2020, p. 8 · the paragraph before it ends with creation stories that "commence with the beginning of time"

Now read who is standing in the sentence. Kings (Denmark, Britain, France's Clovis). Settlers and immigrants — Austria's "Siedler/innen", Canada's Europeans, Australia's convicts. Fathers: the United States has a "Father of Our Country" and "Founding Fathers"; Canada has "Fathers of Confederation". Victors and occupiers: Austria's Second Republic is "again proclaimed" because "the victors had already decided during the war"; Germany's 1949 is asked as a passive ("Wann wurde… gegründet?") and its founding agents, where the bank names any, are the "Alliierte Besatzungsmächte" and, for 1990, the Länder that "acceded." A crowd, once: "les Parisiens." Voters, once: Spain's "88 %." And the people who were already there — in three settler documents, in the sentence before the founding sentence, never in it. Australia's 65,000 years and the First Fleet are adjacent paragraphs that do not touch. Canada's page 14 gives the corpus its only two-subject founding verb — "Aboriginals and Europeans… laid the foundations of Canada" — and page 18 gives the birth to the Fathers alone. The American bank asks who lived in America "before the Europeans arrived" one item after it asks why the colonists came, and the colonists arrive with six motives, all of them honourable.

Then look at what is tested. The narrated founding is old and never examined; the tested founding is a modern document. Denmark narrates a thousand years and prints no questions. Estonia narrates the glacial period and sets twenty-four sample questions with no history in them. Austria's one dated founding item is 1955 — the Staatsvertrag and the end of occupation — and its national day, 26 October, commemorates neither 1918 nor 1945 but the day parliament "resolved the perpetual neutrality of the country." Spain's manual tests 1978 twice (the year, and the meaning of 6 December) and tests 12 October only as a date: item 5033 asks which day is the Fiesta Nacional and no line in the manual says what it commemorates. Germany's catalogue names the Grundgesetz in ten items and never asks when it was adopted — "Verabschiedung des Grundgesetzes" appears once, as a wrong answer for 27 January. The oldest date in the German catalogue is not in the history block at all: item 59, one of the 2024 additions, asks how many years ago the first Jewish community existed on today's German territory, and the largest option is "about 1,700 years." Before 1933, the German state offers its new citizens one date, and it belongs to a synagogue.

And one guide does something none of the others attempts. Estonia's booklet tells the applicant that the founding year is contested — "Russia recognises it as a new country formed… in 1991" — names the disputant, and then, in the same paragraph, explains that the founding date is the reason the reader is holding the booklet: citizenship by birth belongs to those "living in Estonia on 16 June 1940 and their direct descendants. All others must apply." The Estonian-language edition adds a detail the English drops: the 1918 declaration was a "Manifesto to all the peoples of Estonia" — rahvastele, plural. The founding document was addressed to peoples; the applicant's edition loses the title.

4 Credit where due

Estonia's guide earns the credit line for this slot: it is the only document in the corpus that tells the applicant the founding year is disputed, says by whom, and states plainly that the founding date is why the reader must naturalise at all. It then declines to grade any of it — twenty-four sample questions, none on history. Canada takes the runner-up line for the only founding sentence in the corpus with two subjects ("Aboriginals and Europeans… laid the foundations"), and Australia a line for printing "creation stories commence with the beginning of time" as a belief the state reports without arbitrating. The credit is paid before the Limits note what the same three pages do next.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

Deep-time openings are genre, not signature: every narrating guide begins in prehistory or antiquity (Stone Age, glacial period, Celts and Romans, 65,000 years, Angantyr around 700). What is signature is the exception — a question bank that starts in 1933 when the other banks reach back to 1776 and 1252; a European history table with no Romans, Visigoths, 711 or 1812 in it; a national day that commemorates neutrality; a founding date that doubles as a filter.

Banks cannot open with a sentence, so the German, American and Spanish rows compare earliest correct years, not prose. The German catalogue publishes no key: its options are quoted, never asserted as the state's answer. Estonia's credit stands next to a hard fact — the 1940 cut-off is also the reason a large stateless population exists — which belongs to chapter 10.

France is provisional in this ladder: the booklet is a scan without a text layer, and its founding pages were transcribed from page images by a research pass, not yet eye-checked; no zero-counts are printed for it. The UK re-typeset reorders the history sections and its TSO page numbers are pending. Canada's residential-schools sentence, Australia's apology-as-URL and Denmark's Greenland belong to chapters 3 and 5 and are cross-referenced, not repeated. Russia's demos (special voice) both start their history arc with Prince Vladimir baptising Rus' at the end of the tenth century — a sacrament, in Kyiv (2023 demo Q22; 2025 citizenship demo Q1); the citizenship specification's codifier runs 862 → 1.26 "the start of the Special Military Operation" (ch. 6).
