Part II · The past · Chapter 4

The war

Item 164 of the German naturalization catalogue asks what happened on 9 November 1938 and offers four sentences. One of them reads: "With the attack on Poland the Second World War begins." It is the wrong answer. It is also the only sentence in 460 questions that mentions the attack on Poland at all — and even it has no attacker in it. Two hundred kilometres south-east, Austria's booklet tells its applicants that "almost 700,000 Austrians joined the NSDAP," and never once uses the word Opfer. This chapter reads how ten states tell the people they are about to admit what they did, or had done to them, between 1938 and 1945.

The question · agency and testability

When the state was the perpetrator, the collaborator, or the occupied — who acts in the sentence, and does the exam ask?

1 What happened

  • Of the ten core documents, one is the perpetrator state's (DE), one was annexed and supplied soldiers and party members (AT), two were occupied and governed under collaboration (DK, FR), one was occupied twice and mobilised into both armies (EE), four are Allied narrators (UK, CA, US, AU), and one was a neutral dictatorship (ES).
  • Roughly six million Jews were murdered in the Holocaust; the Second World War killed on the order of 60 million people. Which of these numbers appears in a citizenship guide, and in what grammar, is the chapter's subject.
  • Two of the ten documents are question banks (DE, US) and one a hybrid (ES); the German bank publishes no answer key. Its options are quoted below as the applicant sees them, never keyed.
  • The Danish textbook prints no sample questions; the Estonian guide prints 24, none on history; the Austrian booklet prints sample questions with a key. Testability is therefore uneven by design, and the Limits weigh it.

2 The ladder

Ten self-portraits, ordered by how much of the state's own role survives into the sentence: perpetrators named and counted at the top; a collaborator with a stated motive; a collaborator with a fraction; a doubly-occupied state that names its own SS division; the perpetrator state that only tests; then the good war with a "Regrettably", the good war without one, the war as a list, the war as a migration wave, and the war that isn't there.

3 The tell

Take the sentence for 1 September 1939 and read only its subject. Austria: "der Überfall Deutschlands auf Polen" — the state attacks. Denmark: "Hitlers angreb på Polen." Britain: "when Hitler invaded Poland." Canada: "Adolf Hitler… invaded Poland." Estonia: "September 1939 saw the outbreak of World War II" — a month is the subject; nobody attacks anybody. Germany: the sentence exists once, as a distractor, and even there as a nominalisation with no agent — "the attack on Poland". The United States, Australia, Spain, Russia's residence-track demo: no beginning at all. Only one document in the corpus makes the state the attacker, and it is the annexed state describing its annexer. Every Allied narrator makes it a man.

"Mit dem Angriff auf Polen beginnt der Zweite Weltkrieg."

DE · Gesamtfragenkatalog, Aufgabe 164 · a wrong answer to "Was passierte am 9. November 1938?" — the only sentence in 460 items in which Poland is attacked, by nobody

Now the word each state uses for its own role. Austria never uses Opfer: not once in 84 pages, and so the "first victim" thesis is not stated anywhere. Its citizens are supporters, members (700,000), soldiers (1.2 million in the Wehrmacht), fellow-travellers, and — forty pages later, in the chapter on 1980s reforms, after same-sex partnership — "Täter/innen." Denmark's verb is samarbejdede, cooperated, and it comes with a purpose clause: "in order to soften the conditions of occupation." Cooperation is then explained a second time as a cause — of the country's low death toll — and its cost is booked forty pages on, in the civics chapter, under the death penalty: 78 sentenced, 46 carried out. France's régime "collabore" and "porte une part de responsabilité": a fraction, attached to a noun. Estonia's Germans "failed to restore Estonia's independence" and then "Nazi repression ensued"; 8,000 people "were killed" — hukati, executed, in the applicant's Estonian — and 10,000 Jewish prisoners "were brought to Estonia" by nobody. Germany's own noun appears once, in item 179: "the unconditional capitulation of Germany." Britain has no own-role sentence at all; Canada has "Regrettably."

Where does the Holocaust live? Denmark gives it a paragraph of its own and is the only document in the corpus that writes "six million" and names the method — "by mass shootings or in gas chambers in extermination camps" — with the perpetrator as the grammatical subject: "myrdede Nazi-Tyskland." Austria names the victims by group and counts them. Estonia counts a thousand of its own Jews and ten thousand brought in from elsewhere, and does not have the word. Canada has the refugees "turned away… in 1939" and not the word. Britain, the United States, Australia and Spain: nothing. And Germany — the perpetrator state — has the word only inside a verb. "Holocaust" occurs in the catalogue in items 96 and 149, both times as the object of leugnen, to deny. The event is presupposed; the taboo is examined. The catalogue tests the dates twice over (items 152 and 155 ask the same question with different distractors; 153 and 154 both ask when the war ended), tests Stauffenberg, tests Willy Brandt's kneeling in Warsaw, tests 27 January and the Stolpersteine, and never carries a number of the dead. Filed where? Item 96 sits between tax and voting items in the "living in Germany" block, not in the history run.

Read against the corpus baseline, this is not simple avoidance. Five of ten documents lack the Holocaust; a question bank cannot narrate; and against the other banks (US: enemies and a president; ES: nothing) the German catalogue is the richest of its genre. Against the Austrian booklet, which is also tested — Beispielfrage 6 asks where the largest concentration camp on Austrian territory was; Beispielfrage 7 requires three correct of four for who was persecuted after the Anschluss — it is the thinnest. Both are true. What the ladder makes visible is the shape: the states that were occupied or annexed publish hard numbers about their own participation, and each withholds one. Austria prints 700,000 party members and no Waldheim. Denmark prints 78 death sentences and no Frikorps volunteers. Estonia prints its 20th SS division in both languages and no local killer. The number a state chooses to print about itself is the self-portrait; the number next to it is the frame.

Sit this · three real items

DE, Aufgabe 164 (above): pick the sentence that describes 9 November 1938 — and reject the true-sounding one about Poland. No key is published. AT, Beispielfrage 7: "Wer wurde nach dem 'Anschluss' von Österreich an Hitler-Deutschland verfolgt? Jüdinnen/Juden · Roma und Sinti · Menschen mit Behinderung · Nationalistinnen/Nationalsozialisten" — three of four are correct; the key on p. 77 silently corrects the distractor's misprint. US, Q81: "Who did the United States fight in World War II?" — one oral answer, three countries, and that is the whole war.

4 Credit where due

Austria's booklet, pages 31–32, handles this slot best in the corpus: victims named by group — Jews, Roma and Sinti, the Slovene and Czech minorities, churchgoers and priests, homosexuals, disabled people, political opponents — and counted; the camp named; the plebiscite called "neither free nor fair"; the supporters, the soldiers and the executed resisters all counted; the war begun by "Deutschland"; Vienna "conquered by Soviet troops and liberated from National Socialism"; and, in the reforms chapter, the state's own citizens and institutions called perpetrators, with the restitution list that followed. And it is examined. Denmark's page 40 is the runner-up for the only "six million" in the corpus, with the method and the perpetrator as subject. Installments: Estonia for naming its own SS division in both editions; Canada for an apology with a date, an agent and compensation. The credit is paid before the Limits note what each of the four leaves out.

5 Where this stops being true

Limits

Genre carries part of the ladder. Two rows are question banks and one a hybrid; a bank has no room for a paragraph, and the American bank's silence on causes and victims is the genre at its thinnest, not a national verdict — the German bank, graded against it, is the fullest of the three. Australia's row is the 2020 trimmed "testable" edition, from which the history part was removed: its zeros are an editing decision made visible, and the state's choice lay in what to make testable, not in what to say. Denmark's exam items are unseen (the textbook prints none), and Estonia prints twenty-four with no history in them, so "tested" is unequal by design.

The credit cells carry their own frames. Austria's ledger has no Opfer and no Waldheim; Denmark's has no Danish volunteers in German uniform and no name for the camp its 500 deportees were sent to; Estonia's names a division and no killer, and books a murdered minority as a percentage rise ("the proportion of Estonians in the population had risen to 95 percent"); Canada's "Regrettably" makes "public opinion in B.C." the cause and puts the federal government in a by-phrase. Britain is the only document to name Hiroshima — and it enters as a British scientific credit; the same booklet has no internment, no Holocaust, and leaves India off its Allied list one page after counting "more than a million Indians" in the First World War.

France is provisional: the booklet is a scan without a text layer; only "une part de responsabilité" was recorded in the corpus recon, and the surrounding Vichy sidebar was transcribed from a page image and awaits eye-check (the page footer reads "Edition mai 2026", against the July date in our corpus list — a seam to reconcile). No zero-counts are printed for it. The Netherlands and Latvia — natural rows for this slot — publish no book (ch. 11); Japan, the war's other perpetrator state, publishes no self-portrait at all. Russia's 18-page residence-track demo (special voice) contains no Great Patriotic War — its only "Patriotic War" is 1812, and "1945" appears once, as a wrong answer about the constitution — but the 2025 citizenship demo tests the war four times, with an open-answer item on the Panfilov heroes and a syllabus naming sixteen heroes (ch. 6).
